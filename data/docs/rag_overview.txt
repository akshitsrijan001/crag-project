Retrieval-Augmented Generation: An Overview

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a technique introduced by Lewis et al. in their landmark 2020 paper "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks." The core idea is to combine the strengths of pre-trained language models with an external knowledge retrieval mechanism. Instead of relying solely on the parametric knowledge stored in a language model's weights, RAG retrieves relevant documents from an external corpus and conditions the generation on both the input query and the retrieved documents.

The architecture consists of two main components: a retriever and a generator. The retriever, typically based on dense passage retrieval (DPR), encodes both queries and documents into dense vector representations and finds the most relevant documents using similarity search. The generator, usually a sequence-to-sequence model like BART or T5, then takes both the original query and the retrieved documents as input to produce the final answer.

RAG addresses a fundamental limitation of large language models: their knowledge is frozen at training time. By augmenting generation with real-time retrieval, RAG systems can access up-to-date information, provide source attribution for their answers, and reduce the tendency to generate factually incorrect statements. This makes RAG particularly valuable for knowledge-intensive tasks such as open-domain question answering, fact verification, and information synthesis.

The original RAG paper demonstrated two variants: RAG-Sequence, which uses the same retrieved document to generate the complete output sequence, and RAG-Token, which can use different documents for each output token. Both variants showed significant improvements over purely parametric models on benchmarks like Natural Questions, TriviaQA, and WebQuestions.
